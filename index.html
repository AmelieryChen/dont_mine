An educational briefing · American Samoa · 2026

Don’t mine the moana.

The United States is preparing the first commercial deep-sea mining lease sale in U.S. waters — more than 31 million acres of seafloor off American Samoa. The islands have already banned this industry in their own waters. Federal leasing would still put the ocean that feeds, protects, and defines Samoan life at risk.

What deep-sea mining actually does

This is not a quarry on land. It is a plan to vacuum potato-sized polymetallic nodules — rocks that take millions of years to form — from abyssal plains thousands of meters down, then pump them to a surface ship. The industry does not yet exist at commercial scale in U.S. waters.2

Why this is an American Samoa story

Samoa is not a convenient patch of empty ocean. It is a living culture whose identity, food system, and private economy are bound to the moana — the ocean. Federal leasing would happen beyond the three-nautical-mile territorial sea, where Washington, not Pago Pago, holds the legal pen.47

“For generations, our ancestors stewarded these waters with care and restraint. Deep-seabed mining would harm the ecosystems that sustain us, endangering our measina — species sacred to the Samoan culture.”
— Seumalu Dr. Elora Raymond, Faʻasao Amerika Samoa2
“The moana is so much more than a resource. It feeds us. It connects our islands. It carries our culture, our history, and our responsibility to those who come after us.”
— Dr. Sabrina Suluai-Mahuka, Finafinau, radio message to NOAA ship Okeanos Explorer, September 20263

Faʻa Samoa and the ocean as kin

Faʻa Samoa — the Samoan way — organizes life around family, village, and reciprocal care. The ocean is not a warehouse of metals. It is a relative: a source of fish, ceremony, navigation memory, and protection. Community groups describe whales, turtles, and other marine life as measina, treasures of the culture, not surplus biomass.2

When Impossible Metals, a California company, signaled interest in nodules near the territory, fishers, chiefs, faith leaders, and diaspora members organized. In July 2024, then-Governor Lemanu Peleti Mauga signed Executive Order 006-2024: a moratorium on mining, extracting, or exploring for seabed minerals in American Samoa’s own waters.74

That ban still stands. It only covers about three nautical miles from shore. Beyond that line, the U.S. exclusive economic zone stretches 200 nautical miles. That is where the federal lease sale is aimed.4

An economy that already lives on tuna

Tuna fishing and canning are the backbone of the private economy. Canned tuna was about 93% of the territory’s commodity exports in 2017.8 StarKist’s cannery in Pago Pago is the last remaining cannery in American Samoa. Greenpeace USA reported in September 2026 that it employs about 2,450 people and supports an estimated 4,500 jobs in a community of roughly 55,000.3

Governor Pulaaliʻi Nikolao Pula has warned that untested extraction could harm that tuna-dependent economy.3 Mining waste plumes would not have to reach the reef to matter. Skipjack, yellowfin, and the baitfish and mesopelagic prey they follow use the same water column that a discharge plume would cloud.6

A new mineral industry is often sold as “jobs.” The lease stipulations talk about hiring locally and using the Port of Pago Pago.1 Those are promises stacked against a proven industry that already feeds thousands of families — and against cultural harm that a royalty check cannot repair.

The lease is enormous next to the islands

American Samoa’s land area is about 76.8 square miles.8 The proposed sale is more than 31 million acres — on the order of 48,000 square miles, hundreds of times the land area of the islands, and a huge slice of the surrounding ocean. One block sits off eastern Taʻū. The area borders the Cook Islands’ EEZ and lies roughly 57 miles from Rose Atoll National Wildlife Refuge and Marine National Monument.1

Land of American Samoa (schematic)

Proposed lease acreage, drawn at a compressed visual scale. The real ratio is about 630:1.

The National Marine Sanctuary of American Samoa already protects 13,581 square miles of reef and open ocean, including waters at Fagatele Bay, Aunuʻu, Taʻū, Swains, and Muliāva / Rose Atoll — among the most remote sites in the U.S. sanctuary system.9 Mining next door is not a separate planet. Currents, whales, and tuna do not honor lease lines.

What the science already shows

Industry language calls this “preliminary.” Ecology does not. Nodules are habitat. Recovery, where it happens at all, is measured in decades to millennia — not election cycles.

Life in the tracks collapses

After a 2022 industrial collector trial in the eastern Pacific that recovered more than 3,000 tonnes of nodules, scientists measured a 37% drop in sediment-dwelling animal density and a 32% drop in species richness inside the tracks, two months later.10 Plume-affected communities changed which species dominated — a quieter form of biodiversity loss.

Forty-four years is not “recovery”

A Nature study of a decades-old test-mining track found that many biological impacts were still detectable 44 years later, even as some groups had begun to re-establish on a physically altered seafloor.5 That was a tiny experiment, not a 20-year commercial lease.

The midwater is not empty

Discharge plumes can disrupt midwater food webs. A 2025 Nature Communications paper warned that waste particles can dilute or replace the food of filter feeders and impair predation and bioluminescent communication in the twilight zone — the same vertical world that ultimately feeds tuna.6

Protected species are in the path

Earthjustice’s August 18, 2026 lawsuit argues that the National Marine Fisheries Service wrongly concluded leasing is “not likely to adversely affect” ESA-listed whales, sea turtles, corals, and other species. Plaintiffs say the review looked mainly at early surveys, not mining itself.2

Most of this ocean is still unknown

NOAA has spent on the order of $20 million mapping more than 30,000 square nautical miles and confirming nodules are present.1 Mapping metals is not the same as understanding ecosystems. Community leaders have said they welcome science that protects the deep — not science whose purpose is to locate ore.3

The Pacific has said no before

Fiji, Palau, Samoa, Tuvalu, Vanuatu, Kanaky (New Caledonia), and the Federated States of Micronesia have called for moratoriums. Māʻohi Nui (French Polynesia) and Hawaiʻi have banned seabed mining in their own waters.3 American Samoa’s local ban is part of that Pacific stand, not an isolated preference.

How we got here

A local moratorium. A federal override. A lawsuit. A ship in the harbor. This is not a hypothetical from a distant decade.

July 24, 2024

Governor Lemanu Peleti Mauga signs EO 006-2024, a moratorium on seabed mineral exploration and extraction in territorial waters, citing irreversible risks to biodiversity and communities.7

2025

Impossible Metals files an unsolicited lease request. President Trump’s 2025 executive orders push faster access to offshore critical minerals. The Metals Company USA also files NOAA applications for high-seas nodules under a separate U.S. statute — a parallel track, not the American Samoa OCS sale.111

2025–2026

BOEM / the Marine Minerals Administration issues a Request for Information. More than 76,000 comments arrive, overwhelmingly against leasing. The identified area is then expanded from about 18 million acres toward 33 million, with more than 31 million acres later offered in two sale blocks.24

July 17, 2026

Proposed Leasing Notice for PACM-1: sale date November 19, 2026; auction in Camarillo, California; $3 million minimum bid per block; 20-year leases; royalties of 2% then 5%.12

August 18, 2026

Faʻasao Amerika Samoa and Conservation Council for Hawaiʻi, represented by Earthjustice, sue NMFS in the District of Hawaiʻi over the Endangered Species Act consultation.2

September 2, 2026

Finafinau, community leaders, and Greenpeace USA meet NOAA’s Okeanos Explorer in Pago Pago Harbor with “Don’t Mine the Moana” banners, objecting to mineral-mapping work tied to a leasing agenda.3

What you can do

American Samoa cannot out-lobby a federal mineral rush alone. Amplify local organizations, follow the court case, and treat “it’s only exploration” with the skepticism community leaders have asked for.

Follow the lawsuit

Read Earthjustice’s case for Faʻasao Amerika Samoa and Conservation Council for Hawaiʻi, and share primary reporting instead of rumors.

Listen to Finafinau

Dr. Sabrina Suluai-Mahuka’s message to the Okeanos Explorer is a clear statement of welcome-for-science, rejection-of-extraction.

Cite the moratorium

EO 006-2024 is public record. When someone says the islands have not taken a position, point to the order.
